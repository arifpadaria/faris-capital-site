The Number Behind the Robot Demo

A robot lab says 66 percent. Another says 56. A third scores partial credit. Five checks I run on any robotics number before I believe it, with the public cases behind each.

Seven successes in ten trials sounds like a 70 percent robot. The 95 percent confidence interval runs from 35 to 93 percent. Most robotics announcements never say whether the number came from ten trials or a hundred.

They arrive as one percentage and a video. I have spent the past few weeks reading recent ones, along with the reports and papers that test them, and the same thing keeps happening. The headline gives the numerator. The denominator, everything the number counts or leaves out, sits somewhere else, if it appears at all.

Start with what counts as success. Figure reported that its Helix 2.5 model completed 56 percent of tasks in 30 homes it had never seen, against 9 percent for the same system trained from scratch. The only difference was pretraining on Index, Figure's dataset of human behavior. Its scoring rule is strict: "Success required completing the entire task... We give no partial credit." A rollout that needs a human to step in for safety counts as a failure.

Physical Intelligence scores most of its tasks on a rubric. In its π0 paper, a full success scores 1.0 and a partial success earns a fraction. Bussing a table, for example, is scored as the share of objects placed in the right bin. Its π0.5 paper reports the percent of rubric points earned, averaged over 10 trials per task. Both labs do serious work. In one, success is all or nothing. In the other, it is partial credit. Their numbers do not belong on the same chart.

Skild AI reported that its S1 model scored 66 percent on unseen tasks, against 9 percent for a language-prompted baseline. The metric is the average of cumulative per-step success on tasks that run 4 to 8 minutes. Skild says it used human intervention to recover from failures during rollouts, mainly for the baseline, because otherwise the baseline "scores zero." Skild deserves credit for saying so. The post does not give trial counts, and it also shows a policy post-trained on 2,000 demonstrations reaching 86 percent, above the 66 from one video. The 66 is a strong result for what it measures. It is not the share of jobs finished without help.

Then there is the question of who decides when a trial is over. The Toyota Research Institute ran about 1,800 real-world and 47,000 simulated rollouts to test its large behavior models. In simulation, a timeout ends each trial automatically. In the real world, operators decide when a robot has stalled. The authors applied the simulation timeout to the real rollouts. A single-task policy for turning a mug upright fell from 84 percent to 46. A kiwi-placing policy fell from 44 percent to 20. The fine-tuned model on the mug task fell from 88 percent to 50. In the real world, the operator's patience is part of the score. The authors note that discretionary timeouts let real policies make mistakes and recover.

Then there is how many times anyone ran it. TRI used 50 real-world rollouts per task, per policy, per condition, and still warned that "there is significant risk that many robotics papers are measuring statistical noise due to insufficient statistical power." The arithmetic backs them up. Run that 70 percent over 50 trials and the interval narrows to 55 to 82. The π0.5 paper averages 10 trials per task. The Figure and Skild posts give no trial counts at all. Without the count, you cannot tell a result from luck.

Where the test happened matters too. Epoch AI rates robot tasks on four dimensions: reliability, speed, cost and transfer to new settings. Its rule on the last one: "Unless transfer is explicitly shown, it should not be assumed." Demos rarely test it. Figure's evaluation is a good model here. No data was collected in any evaluation home, and the test objects were held out. Even so, Figure writes that "the point is not that general humanoid robotics is solved." The tasks were specified with fine-tuning data collected elsewhere.

The gap between lab and home is wide. The Stanford HAI 2026 AI Index says robots succeed in only 12 percent of real household tasks, while simulated manipulation on RLBench has reached 89.4 percent. On BEHAVIOR-1K, a simulated benchmark of 1,000 household activities, the top team earned a partial-credit score of about 26 percent but completed 12.4 percent of full tasks. Those are two different measurements, and the second one shows what partial credit can hide.

Last is the deployment bar. Per-step success compounds. At 95 percent per step, a robot finishes a 10-step task 60 percent of the time and a 20-step task 36 percent of the time. At 99 percent per step, it finishes 10 steps 90 percent of the time. A strong per-step number can still be a weak task number.

Epoch shows where the bar sits in practice. It classes warehouse picking as the one task in production, with Amazon Robotics' Vulcan and the Boston Dynamics Stretch as examples. Based on interviews with roboticists, Epoch puts reliability in those deployments above 99 percent, possibly 99.9. The same report finds robots typically 3 to 10 times slower than people. It adds that a robot working 20 hours a day makes up for being slower per task. The two numbers need to be read together.

Self-driving has been here before. Apple's California filings showed about one mile per disengagement in 2018 and 118 in 2019. Bloomberg noted that Apple also significantly cut its testing miles. A better ratio over fewer miles says less than it appears to.

The progress is real. Figure's jump from 9 to 56 percent came from pretraining on Index alone, with task data, architecture and training held fixed. Skild's model learned a long task from one demonstration, where its post-trained baseline used up to 2,000. Epoch reports that in Toyota's comparison a pretrained model matched or beat single-task specialists on nearly all tasks. The trend is steep.

My point is narrower. An investor has to price the distance between the number on the slide and the number a customer needs. A signed customer contract is the one number that comes with its denominator attached.

So I now ask five things of any robotics founder showing a result. How is success defined, and does a human reset count as a zero? Who decides when a trial ends, a fixed timeout or an operator? How many trials, with what error bars, in how many places, and were any of them somewhere the robot had never seen? What is the completion rate over the full sequence, not per step? And how fast is it, and for how many hours a day? A slow robot needs long uptime to pay back.

Which of the five did the last demo you watched answer? Thoughts?
